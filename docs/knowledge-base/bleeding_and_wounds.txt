# Severe Bleeding and Wound Care
Source: Adapted from WHO first aid guidance and ICRC first aid principles (authored summary, not verbatim)

## Immediate Control of Bleeding
Apply firm, direct pressure on the wound with the cleanest cloth available — a shirt, towel, or gauze. Press down hard and do not lift the cloth to check if bleeding has stopped; if blood soaks through, add another layer on top rather than removing the first. Keep applying pressure continuously for at least 10 to 15 minutes. If possible, raise the injured limb above the level of the heart while maintaining pressure, unless you suspect a broken bone.

## When to Use a Tourniquet
A tourniquet is a last resort for severe limb bleeding that will not stop with direct pressure and is life-threatening. Tie a wide band (a belt, folded cloth, or bandage — never wire or thin cord) about 5 to 7 centimeters above the wound, not directly on a joint. Tighten until bleeding stops. Write down the exact time it was applied on the person's skin or clothing with a pen. A tourniquet left on too long can cause permanent damage, so the injured person must reach a hospital as fast as possible.

## Signs of Shock from Blood Loss
Watch for pale or grey skin, cold and clammy touch, rapid weak pulse, fast shallow breathing, confusion, or fainting. Lay the person flat, raise their legs about 30 centimeters if there is no suspected spinal or leg injury, keep them warm with a blanket or clothing, and do not give them food or water. Get them to medical help immediately — shock can be fatal within a short time.

## Cleaning Minor Wounds
For small cuts and scrapes once bleeding has stopped, rinse the wound gently with clean or boiled-and-cooled water to remove dirt. Avoid using strong antiseptics like iodine directly in a deep wound. Cover with a clean, dry cloth or bandage and change it daily or if it becomes wet or dirty. Watch for signs of infection over the following days: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, red streaks spreading from the wound, or fever — these mean the person needs medical attention.

## What Not To Do
Do not remove any large object impaled in a wound — stabilize it in place with padding around it and get medical help. Do not apply ice directly to broken skin. Do not use butter, oil, mud, ash, or toothpaste on a wound, as these increase infection risk. Do not try to push protruding organs back into an abdominal wound; cover loosely with a clean, damp cloth instead and seek emergency care immediately.
