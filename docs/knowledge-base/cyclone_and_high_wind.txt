# Cyclone, Hurricane, and High Wind Safety
Source: Adapted from India Meteorological Department cyclone guidance and NDMA guidelines (authored summary)

## Before a Cyclone Arrives
Follow official warnings and evacuation orders immediately — cyclones are tracked well in advance, and early evacuation is far safer than waiting. Secure or bring indoors loose outdoor items such as furniture, tin sheets, and signage that could become dangerous projectiles in high wind. Board up or tape large windows, and fill containers with clean drinking water in case supply is disrupted. Charge phones, power banks, and any battery-powered lights fully.

## Choosing a Safe Shelter
If you must stay rather than evacuate, choose the smallest interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows and doors, such as a bathroom or interior hallway. Avoid rooms with wide roof spans like a living room, since these are more likely to lose their roof in extreme wind. Stay away from coastal areas, riverbanks, and low-lying ground due to storm surge and flash flooding risk, which often causes more deaths than the wind itself.

## During the Storm
Stay indoors and away from windows throughout the storm. Do not go outside during a lull or the passing of the eye of a cyclone — winds can return suddenly and violently from the opposite direction. Listen to a battery-powered or hand-crank radio for official updates if cellular networks are down.

## Storm Surge and Coastal Flooding
Storm surge — a rise in sea level pushed inland by the storm — can flood coastal areas rapidly and is often the deadliest part of a cyclone. If you are in a coastal or low-lying area under an evacuation order, leave well before the storm arrives; do not wait to see how bad it gets.

## After the Cyclone Passes
Stay indoors until authorities confirm it is safe — the storm may not be fully over even after winds die down. Watch for downed power lines, damaged buildings, and flooding when you do go out, and avoid clearing debris near power lines yourself. Boil or purify drinking water until you know the supply is safe, since cyclones often contaminate water sources and disrupt water treatment.
