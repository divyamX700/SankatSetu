# Psychological First Aid and Mental Health in a Crisis
Source: Adapted from WHO Psychological First Aid guide and IASC mental health and psychosocial support guidelines (authored summary)

## What Psychological First Aid Is
Psychological first aid means offering practical care and support to someone recently affected by a crisis, in a way that respects their dignity and does not force them to talk about what happened. It is not professional counseling or therapy — it is basic human support: being present, listening, helping meet immediate practical needs, and connecting people to further help and information. Anyone can offer it; no special medical training is required.

## Look, Listen, Link
A simple framework for psychological first aid: **Look** — observe for people with obvious urgent basic needs (injury, unsafe situations) and people who appear extremely distressed and may need more support. **Listen** — approach people who need support, ask about their needs and concerns, and listen without pressuring them to talk. **Link** — help people access basic needs like food, water, and information, help them connect with loved ones and social support, and connect them with further help such as medical care or protection services if needed.

## What to Say and Do
Stay calm, speak gently, and be patient. Acknowledge what has happened honestly rather than making false promises like "everything will be fine." Give practical help such as water, a blanket, or accurate information if you have it. Let the person express emotions like crying or anger without judgment; this is a normal response to an abnormal situation, not a sign of weakness or a disorder. Help them feel safe and give a sense of what is happening and what to expect next, if you know.

## What Not to Do
Do not pressure anyone to share their story or feelings if they don't want to. Do not interrupt or take over the conversation with your own experiences. Do not give false reassurance or minimize what happened ("it could have been worse"). Do not label people's reactions as "crazy" or overreacting. Do not make promises about things outside your control, such as guaranteeing a missing family member is safe.

## Recognizing When Someone Needs More Help
Seek professional mental health support for someone who: cannot care for themselves or their children, talks about wanting to harm themselves or others, is unable to function (cannot eat, sleep, or communicate) days after the event, or shows severe ongoing symptoms like panic attacks, extreme withdrawal, or disorientation about who or where they are. If someone expresses suicidal thoughts, take it seriously, stay with them if safe to do so, remove immediate means of harm if possible, and get them to emergency medical or mental health help as soon as possible — do not leave them alone.

## Supporting Children in a Crisis
Children often look to adults for cues on how to react — staying as calm as possible helps them feel safer. Keep children close to trusted caregivers, maintain simple routines where possible (regular meals, bedtime), and answer their questions honestly but simply, without overwhelming detail. Physical comfort like holding a young child's hand or a hug can help. Watch for regression (bedwetting, thumb-sucking in an older child), clinginess, nightmares, or withdrawal, which are normal short-term reactions but should be discussed with a health worker if they persist for weeks.

## Caring for Yourself as a Helper
Helping others in a crisis is demanding, and helpers can experience their own stress, exhaustion, or trauma. Take breaks, eat, drink water, and rest when possible. Talk to someone you trust about what you have seen and done. Recognize that you cannot help everyone or fix every problem, and that is not a personal failure.
