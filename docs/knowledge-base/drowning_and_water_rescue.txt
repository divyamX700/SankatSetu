# Drowning and Water Rescue
Source: Adapted from ILS/Red Cross water safety and drowning response guidance (authored summary)

## Reach, Throw, Don't Go
If someone is struggling in water, the safest rescue for an untrained person is to avoid entering the water yourself. First try to reach them with a stick, branch, rope, or clothing from the shore or a boat. If that is not possible, throw them something that floats — a rope, empty container, ball, or life jacket. Only enter the water yourself as an absolute last resort and only if you are a strong, trained swimmer, since many drowning deaths happen when a rescuer also gets into trouble.

## After Pulling Someone From Water
Once the person is out of the water, check if they are breathing and responsive. If they are not breathing normally, begin CPR immediately — for a drowning victim, if you are trained, start with a few rescue breaths before chest compressions, since lack of oxygen is usually the primary problem. Continue CPR until they start breathing on their own, emergency help arrives, or you cannot physically continue.

## Secondary Drowning Awareness
Even a person who coughs up water and seems fine after a near-drowning incident should be monitored closely for several hours and seen by a doctor if possible. Water inhaled into the lungs can cause breathing difficulty or complications hours later ("secondary drowning" or delayed pulmonary symptoms) — watch for coughing, difficulty breathing, extreme tiredness, or behavior changes and seek medical care if these appear.

## Preventing Drowning
Never leave young children unsupervised near any water, including buckets, bathtubs, ponds, and pools — drowning can happen silently in seconds with very little water. In flood or open water situations, wear a life jacket if available. Avoid swimming alone, in unfamiliar water, at night, or after consuming alcohol. Be aware of strong currents near riverbanks and coastlines, especially during and after heavy rain.

## Ice and Cold Water Immersion
Cold water immersion can cause sudden gasping and rapid loss of muscle control within minutes, even for strong swimmers, so cold water rescues are especially dangerous for untrained rescuers. If someone falls through ice, do not walk out onto the ice yourself; use the reach-throw-don't-go approach with a rope or long pole, and get emergency help immediately since hypothermia sets in quickly.
