# Severe Bleeding and Wound Care
Source: Adapted from WHO first aid guidance, ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines, and NDMA India first-responder material — authored summary, not verbatim, with citations per section below

## Immediate Control of Bleeding
Apply firm, direct pressure on the wound with the cleanest cloth available — a shirt, towel, or gauze. Press down hard and do not lift the cloth to check if bleeding has stopped; if blood soaks through, add another layer on top rather than removing the first, since removing a soaked dressing can pull away any clot that has started to form. Keep applying pressure continuously for at least 10 to 15 minutes without interruption. If possible, raise the injured limb above the level of the heart while maintaining pressure, unless you suspect a broken bone in that limb. If you have gloves or a plastic bag, use them as a barrier between your hands and the person's blood to protect both of you from infection. [Source: ICRC, "First Aid Guidelines" on wound and bleeding management / WHO basic first aid guidance]

## When to Use a Tourniquet
A tourniquet is a last resort for severe limb bleeding that will not stop with direct pressure and is life-threatening. It is not the first thing to try — keep applying firm direct pressure first, and only move to a tourniquet if bleeding continues to soak through and the person's life is clearly at risk. If no proper tourniquet material is available, continued firm direct pressure with as many hands and as much cloth as needed is a reasonable substitute while transport is arranged. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on tourniquet use for life-threatening limb bleeding]

## How to Apply a Tourniquet
Tie a wide band (a belt, folded cloth, or purpose-made bandage — never wire or thin cord) about 5 to 7 centimeters above the wound, not directly on a joint. Tighten until the bleeding visibly stops, which will be painful for the person; this is expected and correct. Write down the exact time it was applied on the person's skin or clothing with a pen, since this information is critical for the treating hospital. Once applied correctly, a tourniquet should not be loosened or removed by a bystander, even hours later; leave that decision to hospital staff. A tourniquet left on too long can cause permanent limb damage, so the injured person must reach a hospital as fast as possible, but the immediate risk of bleeding to death outweighs the risk of temporary tourniquet use. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on tourniquet application technique]

## Signs of Shock from Blood Loss
Watch for pale or grey skin, cold and clammy touch, rapid weak pulse, fast shallow breathing, confusion, restlessness, extreme thirst, or fainting. Lay the person flat, raise their legs about 30 centimeters if there is no suspected spinal, pelvic, or leg injury, keep them warm with a blanket or clothing, and do not give them food or water in case they need surgery. Loosen tight clothing around the neck, chest, and waist. Talk to the person calmly and reassure them, since fear and pain can worsen the physiological stress response. Get them to medical help immediately — shock from blood loss can become irreversible and fatal within a short time if untreated. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on recognizing and managing shock]

## Cleaning Minor Wounds
For small cuts and scrapes once bleeding has stopped, rinse the wound gently with clean or boiled-and-cooled water to remove dirt and debris, ideally under a gentle running stream rather than by wiping, which can push dirt deeper. Avoid using strong antiseptics like undiluted iodine or spirit directly in a deep or large wound, as they can damage healthy tissue and delay healing; mild soap and water is usually sufficient for the surrounding skin. Cover with a clean, dry cloth or bandage and change it daily or whenever it becomes wet or visibly dirty. Watch for signs of infection over the following days: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, red streaks spreading from the wound toward the heart, or fever — these mean the person needs medical attention, ideally including a check on whether their tetanus vaccination is up to date. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on wound cleaning and infection prevention]

## What Not To Do
Do not remove any large object impaled in a wound — stabilize it in place with padding built up around it on both sides and get medical help, since removal outside a hospital can trigger severe bleeding the object was partly blocking. Do not apply ice directly to broken skin. Do not use butter, oil, mud, ash, turmeric paste, or toothpaste on a wound, as these are not sterile and increase infection risk, a caution that applies to many common household remedies used in rural settings. Do not try to push protruding organs back into an abdominal wound; cover loosely with a clean, damp cloth or plastic wrap instead and seek emergency care immediately, keeping the person lying down with knees bent if that is more comfortable for them. [Source: ICRC first aid guidelines on wound care precautions]

## Nosebleeds
For a common nosebleed, have the person sit upright and lean slightly forward, not backward — leaning back lets blood run down the throat and can cause choking or vomiting. Pinch the soft part of the nose firmly, just below the bony bridge, for a full 10 to 15 minutes without releasing to check. Breathing through the mouth during this time is normal. Applying a cold compress to the bridge of the nose or back of the neck may help. If bleeding continues beyond 20 to 30 minutes of continuous pressure, is unusually heavy, follows a significant head injury, or happens together with easy bruising elsewhere on the body, seek medical care. [Source: WHO/ICRC first aid guidance on minor bleeding management]

## Puncture Wounds and Embedded Small Objects
A puncture wound (from a nail, thorn, or similar) can look small on the surface but carry dirt and bacteria deep into tissue, and carries a notable tetanus risk since it is a deep wound with little bleeding to flush it out. Clean the surrounding skin, allow a little bleeding to help flush the wound if bleeding is not excessive, and cover with a clean dressing. Do not dig for a small deeply embedded object like a splinter tip yourself if it is not easily visible near the surface; leave deeper extraction to a health worker. Anyone with a puncture wound, especially from something that has been in soil or manure, should have their tetanus vaccination status checked as soon as possible. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on puncture wounds and tetanus risk]

## Bleeding in Children and Elderly Persons
Children can appear stable for longer than adults even with significant blood loss, then deteriorate suddenly, so do not assume a calm-looking child is not seriously injured — treat visible bleeding and watch closely for pale skin, unusual sleepiness, or fast breathing. Elderly people, and anyone on blood-thinning medication, may bleed more heavily and for longer from a wound that would be minor in someone else, and are also more prone to shock; apply the same direct-pressure principles but seek medical evaluation sooner rather than waiting to see if bleeding stops on its own. [Source: WHO/ICRC first aid guidance, general pediatric and elderly first aid considerations]
