# Heat Stroke, Heat Exhaustion, and Dehydration
Source: Adapted from WHO heat-health guidance, NDMA India heat wave advisories, and CDC extreme heat guidance — authored summary, with citations per section below

## Heat Exhaustion vs Heat Stroke
Heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, cold or clammy skin, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, and muscle cramps, with body temperature usually normal or only mildly raised. Heat stroke is a medical emergency: body temperature rises above 40°C (104°F), skin becomes hot and dry or occasionally still sweaty, and the person may become confused, slur speech, have a rapid pulse, seize, or lose consciousness. Heat stroke can be fatal within a short time and needs emergency medical help immediately — the key distinguishing feature to watch for is altered mental state (confusion, strange behavior, or unconsciousness) combined with very hot skin, which separates it from simple heat exhaustion. [Source: WHO heat-health guidance / NDMA India heat wave advisory]

## Treating Heat Exhaustion
Move the person to a cool or shaded place immediately. Have them lie down and raise their legs slightly. Loosen or remove excess clothing. Cool their skin with a damp cloth, fan them, and give small, frequent sips of cool water or an oral rehydration solution if they are alert and able to swallow. They should feel better within 30 minutes; if not, or symptoms worsen, treat as heat stroke and seek emergency care without waiting further. [Source: NDMA India heat wave advisory / WHO heat-health guidance]

## Treating Heat Stroke
Call for emergency medical help immediately — this is life-threatening. While waiting, move the person to shade or a cool area, remove excess clothing, and cool them as rapidly as possible: apply cool wet cloths or water to the skin, especially the neck, armpits, and groin where large blood vessels are close to the surface, and fan them continuously to increase evaporative cooling. If ice or cold packs are available, apply them to the same areas, wrapped in cloth to avoid direct skin injury. Do not give an unconscious or confused person anything to drink, due to choking risk. Continue active cooling until emergency responders arrive or the person's condition clearly improves, since rapid cooling in the first period after heat stroke onset significantly affects outcome. [Source: WHO heat-health guidance on managing heat stroke]

## Preventing Heat-Related Illness
During extreme heat, drink water regularly throughout the day even before feeling thirsty, and avoid excess tea, coffee, and alcohol which increase fluid loss through urination. Avoid strenuous outdoor activity during the hottest part of the day, typically late morning through mid-afternoon, and reschedule outdoor labor or travel to early morning or evening where possible. Wear light-colored, loose, breathable clothing and a hat or cloth covering when outdoors, and use shade or an umbrella when direct sun exposure cannot be avoided. Check on elderly neighbours, young children, outdoor workers, and anyone with chronic illness during heat waves, as they are at higher risk and may not recognize their own symptoms early enough. [Source: NDMA India heat wave advisory / WHO heat-health guidance]

## Oral Rehydration for Dehydration
For dehydration from heat, diarrhea, or vomiting, an oral rehydration solution helps the body absorb fluid and salts faster than water alone. If a packaged ORS sachet is not available, mix 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a teaspoon of salt into 1 liter of clean or boiled-and-cooled water. Give small, frequent sips rather than large amounts at once, especially to children, to avoid vomiting, and continue offering fluids even after the person seems settled, since dehydration can recur quickly in continued heat or ongoing illness. [Source: WHO guidance on oral rehydration solution preparation and use]

## Recognizing Dehydration, Especially in Children and Infants
Signs of dehydration include a dry mouth and tongue, reduced or dark yellow urine, sunken eyes, lethargy or unusual irritability, and in infants, a sunken soft spot on the top of the head and fewer wet diapers than usual. Severe dehydration — very little or no urine output, extreme lethargy, cold extremities, or a rapid weak pulse — needs urgent medical care, particularly in infants and young children, who can deteriorate faster than adults because they have smaller fluid reserves relative to their size. Continue breastfeeding an infant through any illness causing dehydration risk, alongside ORS if advised by a health worker. [Source: WHO guidance on recognizing and managing dehydration in children]

## Heat Cramps
Heat cramps are painful, involuntary muscle spasms, often in the legs or abdomen, that occur during or after heavy exertion in hot conditions and heavy sweating. Stop the activity, move to a cool place, stretch and gently massage the affected muscle, and drink water or an oral rehydration solution to replace lost salts. Heat cramps are usually not dangerous on their own but can be an early warning sign of heat exhaustion, so watch for progression to other symptoms and rest fully before resuming activity. [Source: WHO/CDC general heat illness guidance]

## Protecting Outdoor Workers and Vulnerable Groups During Heat Waves
People who work outdoors — farmers, construction and road workers, vendors, and rickshaw or auto drivers — face some of the highest heat exposure risk and should take scheduled water and shade breaks, avoid working alone during extreme heat so a co-worker can notice early symptoms, and know the signs of heat exhaustion in themselves and others. Local heat action plans issued by district authorities or the India Meteorological Department during heat wave warnings often include advice on adjusted work hours; following these official local advisories, alongside general precautions, gives the most locally accurate guidance. [Source: NDMA India heat wave advisory]
