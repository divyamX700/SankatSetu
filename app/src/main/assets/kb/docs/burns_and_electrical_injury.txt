# Burns and Electrical Injury
Source: Adapted from WHO burn care guidance, ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines, and NDMA India first-responder material — authored summary, with citations per section below

## Immediate Cooling of a Burn
Cool the burn under cool (not ice-cold) running water for 20 minutes as soon as possible — this is the single most effective first aid step and reduces pain and depth of injury even if started up to 3 hours after the burn. Remove any jewellery, tight clothing, or watches near the burn before swelling starts, unless they are stuck to the skin, in which case leave them and let medical staff handle removal. Do not use ice, iced water, butter, oil, toothpaste, or ointments on a fresh burn, since ice can worsen tissue damage through cold injury and greasy substances trap heat and complicate medical assessment. If running water is not available, any clean, cool (not frozen) water source can be used, including from a bucket poured continuously over the area. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on burn cooling / ICRC first aid guidelines]

## Covering a Burn
After cooling, cover the burn loosely with a clean, non-fluffy material such as plastic cling film (placed loosely, not wrapped tightly, since a burn continues to swell) or a clean cloth. Do not burst any blisters that form — an intact blister protects the underlying skin from infection while it heals. Do not apply any home remedies, ash, cow dung, herbal pastes, or raw egg to a burn, as these are not sterile and significantly increase infection risk, a caution particularly relevant to some traditional home treatments still used in rural areas. Reassess the wound daily for cleanliness and watch for signs of infection such as increasing redness, pus, or worsening pain. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on burn wound covering and infection prevention]

## When a Burn Needs Hospital Care
Seek medical care immediately if the burn is larger than the person's palm, is on the face, hands, feet, genitals, or a major joint, looks white, brown, or charred (a sign of a deep burn), is caused by chemicals or electricity, or if the person is a young child, elderly, or has difficulty breathing. Burns around the mouth, nose, or from a fire in an enclosed space can mean airway injury from inhaled smoke or superheated air — this is a medical emergency requiring urgent evaluation even without visible burns on the skin, since airway swelling can develop over the following hours and obstruct breathing. [Source: WHO burn care guidance on referral criteria for hospital treatment]

## Electrical Injury Safety
Before touching someone who has had an electric shock, make sure the power source is switched off at the mains or breaker, or that the person is no longer in contact with the live source. If you cannot turn off the power, use a dry, non-conductive object like a wooden stick, dry rope, or dry rubber item to move the person or the wire away — never use your bare hands or anything metal or damp. Once safe to approach, check if the person is breathing and responsive; electrical injury can cause cardiac arrest by disrupting the heart's electrical rhythm, so be ready to start CPR immediately if they are not breathing normally. Electrical burns often look small at the skin's entry and exit points but can cause severe damage to muscle and internal organs along the current's path, so anyone who has had a significant electric shock needs medical evaluation even if they seem fine afterward. [Source: ICRC first aid guidelines on electrical injury / WHO basic first aid guidance]

## Chemical Burns
For a chemical burn, brush off any dry chemical powder first with a gloved hand or cloth before adding water, since some dry chemicals react with water, then flush the area with large amounts of clean running water for at least 20 minutes, removing contaminated clothing and jewellery while flushing. Avoid getting the chemical on yourself during this process. Seek medical help, and if possible identify the chemical involved — keep the container or label — to tell responders, since specific chemicals may need specific treatment beyond flushing. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on chemical burns]

## Sunburn and Minor Heat Burns
For sunburn or a very minor heat burn without blistering, cool the skin with a cool (not cold) compress or shower, apply a plain, fragrance-free moisturizer once the skin has cooled, and keep the area out of further sun exposure while it heals. Give extra fluids, since sunburned skin loses moisture. Blistering sunburn, sunburn covering a large area of the body, or sunburn in an infant should be assessed by a health worker, and painkillers appropriate for the person's age can help with discomfort under general medical guidance. [Source: WHO general first aid guidance on minor burns]

## Special Considerations for Children and Pregnant Women
Children have thinner skin than adults, so a burn from the same heat source and duration can be deeper and more serious in a child, and children also lose body heat and fluid faster relative to their size, increasing shock risk from even a moderate burn — err on the side of seeking medical care sooner for a child's burn. For a pregnant woman with a significant burn, electrical injury, or chemical exposure, urgent medical assessment is important for both her health and the pregnancy, even if she initially feels well, since some exposures can affect the fetus without obvious symptoms in the mother. [Source: WHO burn care guidance, general pediatric and maternal first aid considerations]
