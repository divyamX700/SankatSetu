# Psychological First Aid and Mental Health in a Crisis
Source: Adapted from the WHO/War Trauma Foundation/World Vision International "Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers" and IASC mental health and psychosocial support guidelines — authored summary, with citations per section below

## What Psychological First Aid Is
Psychological first aid means offering practical care and support to someone recently affected by a crisis, in a way that respects their dignity and does not force them to talk about what happened. It is not professional counseling or therapy — it is basic human support: being present, listening, helping meet immediate practical needs, and connecting people to further help and information. Anyone can offer it; no special medical training is required, though being aware of your own limits and knowing when to refer someone to professional help is part of doing it responsibly. [Source: WHO Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers]

## Look, Listen, Link
A simple framework for psychological first aid: Look means observing for people with obvious urgent basic needs (injury, unsafe situations) and people who appear extremely distressed and may need more support, without assuming everyone needs the same level of help. Listen means approaching people who need support, asking about their needs and concerns, and listening without pressuring them to talk or share more than they want to. Link means helping people access basic needs like food, water, and information, helping them connect with loved ones and social support, and connecting them with further help such as medical care or protection services if needed, including for children or others who may need additional protection. [Source: WHO Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers — "Look, Listen, Link" framework]

## What to Say and Do
Stay calm, speak gently, and be patient, since your own composure can visibly help someone in distress feel safer. Acknowledge what has happened honestly rather than making false promises like "everything will be fine." Give practical help such as water, a blanket, or accurate information if you have it, since meeting concrete needs often does more to reduce distress than words alone. Let the person express emotions like crying or anger without judgment; this is a normal response to an abnormal situation, not a sign of weakness or a disorder. Help them feel safe and give a sense of what is happening and what to expect next, if you know, since uncertainty itself is a major source of ongoing distress after a crisis. [Source: WHO Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers]

## What Not to Do
Do not pressure anyone to share their story or feelings if they don't want to — this can retraumatize rather than help. Do not interrupt or take over the conversation with your own experiences. Do not give false reassurance or minimize what happened ("it could have been worse"). Do not label people's reactions as "crazy" or overreacting. Do not make promises about things outside your control, such as guaranteeing a missing family member is safe, since a broken promise can deepen distress and damage trust. [Source: WHO Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers]

## Recognizing When Someone Needs More Help
Seek professional mental health support for someone who cannot care for themselves or their children, talks about wanting to harm themselves or others, is unable to function (cannot eat, sleep, or communicate) days after the event, or shows severe ongoing symptoms like panic attacks, extreme withdrawal, or disorientation about who or where they are. If someone expresses suicidal thoughts, take it seriously, stay with them if safe to do so, remove immediate means of harm if possible, and get them to emergency medical or mental health help as soon as possible — do not leave them alone, and do not assume they are just seeking attention. [Source: WHO Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers / IASC mental health and psychosocial support guidelines]

## Supporting Children in a Crisis
Children often look to adults for cues on how to react — staying as calm as possible in front of them helps them feel safer, even when the adult is also frightened. Keep children close to trusted caregivers, maintain simple routines where possible (regular meals, bedtime), and answer their questions honestly but simply, without overwhelming detail beyond what they asked. Physical comfort like holding a young child's hand or a hug can help when the child is receptive to it. Watch for regression (bedwetting, thumb-sucking in an older child), clinginess, nightmares, or withdrawal, which are normal short-term reactions but should be discussed with a health worker if they persist for several weeks. Involve children in simple, age-appropriate tasks during recovery (like helping pack a bag or care for a younger sibling) where possible, since a sense of usefulness and some control can help counter feelings of helplessness. [Source: IASC guidelines on mental health and psychosocial support for children in emergencies]

## Caring for Yourself as a Helper
Helping others in a crisis is demanding, and helpers — including community members, volunteers, and family members caring for others — can experience their own stress, exhaustion, or trauma, sometimes called secondary traumatic stress. Take breaks, eat, drink water, and rest when possible, even when it feels like there is no time. Talk to someone you trust about what you have seen and done, rather than carrying it alone. Recognize that you cannot help everyone or fix every problem, and that is not a personal failure — sustainable help over days and weeks requires pacing yourself, not just an intense initial effort. [Source: WHO Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers, section on helper self-care]

## Grief and Loss After a Disaster
Grief after losing a loved one, a home, or a way of life in a disaster can include a wide range of normal reactions: shock, denial, anger, guilt, deep sadness, or periods of numbness, and these can come in waves rather than a fixed, predictable sequence. There is no fixed "correct" timeline for grief, and different family members, including children, may grieve visibly differently from each other. Supporting someone in grief mainly means being present, allowing them to talk about their loss if they want to (including about the person or place they lost, not only the practical crisis response), and helping with practical tasks, rather than trying to fix their feelings or rush them toward "moving on." Rituals, including religious or cultural mourning practices, can be an important and appropriate part of coping and should be supported and facilitated where possible rather than treated as an obstacle to recovery logistics. [Source: IASC guidelines on mental health and psychosocial support, general grief support principles consistent with WHO psychological first aid guidance]

## Community and Cultural Considerations in India
Psychological first aid should be adapted respectfully to local language, customs, gender norms, and family structure — for example, being mindful of who a person may feel comfortable being alone with or speaking in front of, and respecting joint family decision-making dynamics where they are relevant to a person's practical concerns. Local community leaders, religious figures, and trusted local health workers such as ASHA workers are often well placed to provide culturally appropriate psychological first aid alongside formal responders, since existing trust relationships matter greatly during a crisis. Where possible, connect people with locally available government or NGO mental health helplines and services rather than assuming no such support exists. [Source: IASC guidelines on mental health and psychosocial support, general principles for culturally appropriate psychological first aid]
