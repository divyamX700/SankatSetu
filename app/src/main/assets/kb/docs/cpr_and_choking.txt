# CPR, Cardiac Arrest, and Choking
Source: Adapted from ICRC/IFRC first aid guidance and WHO basic life support principles — authored summary, with citations per section below

## Recognizing Cardiac Arrest
If a person is unresponsive — not moving, not reacting to being shaken firmly or shouted at — and not breathing normally (no breathing at all, or only occasional irregular gasping known as agonal breathing), treat this as cardiac arrest. Call for emergency help or ask someone nearby to call 112 immediately, and start CPR without delay — do not wait to see if the person "comes around." Every minute without CPR or defibrillation reduces the chance of survival significantly, so acting fast matters more than acting perfectly. If you are alone with no phone, and the person is an adult, it is generally recommended to call for help first and then start CPR, since getting emergency responders moving early is critical; for a child or infant, give about 2 minutes of CPR first if you are truly alone, then call for help. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on recognizing cardiac arrest]

## Performing Chest Compressions
Kneel beside the person on a firm, flat surface. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, between the nipples, and your other hand on top with fingers interlocked. Keep your arms straight and your shoulders directly over your hands, and push hard and fast, at least 5 centimeters deep, at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute — roughly the tempo of the song "Stayin' Alive." Allow the chest to fully rise and recoil between compressions, since incomplete recoil reduces blood flow back into the heart. If you are trained in rescue breaths, give 30 compressions followed by 2 breaths, tilting the head back and lifting the chin to open the airway for each breath; if untrained or unwilling to give breaths, continuous hands-only chest compressions without pauses are still highly effective and far better than doing nothing. For a child (from about age one to puberty), use one or both hands and compress about one-third the depth of the chest; for the technique for infants under one year, see the choking and infant CPR guidance below, which uses two fingers instead. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on chest compression technique / WHO basic life support principles]

## Continuing CPR
Do not stop CPR until emergency medical help arrives and takes over, an automated external defibrillator (AED) becomes available and is used, the person starts breathing normally and moving on their own, or you are physically too exhausted to continue safely. If multiple people are present, switch who is doing compressions every 2 minutes, with as little pause as possible during the switch, since compression quality drops sharply with fatigue even before the rescuer notices it themselves. If an AED becomes available, turn it on and follow its spoken instructions exactly — it will analyze the heart rhythm and tell you whether a shock is advised; stand clear of the person while it analyzes and during the shock itself. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on sustained CPR and AED use]

## Choking in Adults and Children Over One Year
Ask the person "Are you choking?" If they can cough, speak, or breathe, encourage them to keep coughing forcefully — do not intervene, and do not slap their back while they can still cough effectively, since this can dislodge the object at an unpredictable moment. If they cannot cough, speak, or breathe, or are making a high-pitched wheezing sound, stand behind them, lean them well forward so gravity can help, and give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. If this does not clear the blockage, perform abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich maneuver): stand behind the person, wrap your arms around their waist, make a fist just above their navel, grasp it with your other hand, and pull sharply inward and upward. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object is expelled or the person becomes unresponsive. For a visibly pregnant woman or a person who is significantly overweight, give chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts, positioning your fist on the center of the breastbone rather than the abdomen. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on choking in adults and children]

## Choking in Infants Under One Year
Never perform abdominal thrusts on an infant, since this risks serious internal injury to their smaller, less protected organs. Lay the infant face-down along your forearm, supporting the head and jaw firmly with your hand, with the head lower than the chest, and rest your forearm on your thigh for stability. Give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. If unsuccessful, turn the infant face-up along your other arm, still keeping the head lower than the chest, and give up to 5 chest thrusts using two fingers on the center of the chest, just below the nipple line — similar in position to infant CPR compressions but sharper and more separated. Alternate back blows and chest thrusts until the object clears or the infant becomes unresponsive, in which case begin infant CPR (two-finger chest compressions at the same landmark, about 4 centimeters deep) and get emergency help immediately. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on infant choking and infant basic life support]

## If the Person Becomes Unresponsive While Choking
Lower them carefully to the ground, call for emergency help immediately, and begin CPR, starting with chest compressions. Each time you open the airway to give a rescue breath, look inside the mouth for the object and remove it only if you can clearly see and reach it with your fingers — do not do a blind finger sweep of the mouth, as this can push the object deeper into the airway and make the obstruction worse. Continue full CPR cycles until the object is expelled, the person begins breathing on their own, or emergency responders take over. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on unresponsive choking victims]

## Special Considerations for Pregnant Women and People with Injuries
When giving chest compressions to a woman in the later stages of visible pregnancy who is lying flat, if possible have a helper gently tilt her hips slightly to one side using a rolled blanket or their hands under one side of her back, which can improve blood flow to the heart during CPR; do not delay starting compressions to arrange this. For a person with a suspected chest, rib, or spinal injury, CPR still takes priority over concern for further injury if they are in cardiac arrest, since without CPR they will not survive regardless — broken ribs from correctly performed compressions are a known and accepted risk of effective CPR. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines, general adaptations for special populations]

## Recovery Position for a Breathing but Unresponsive Person
If a person is unresponsive but breathing normally, do not leave them lying flat on their back, since their tongue or vomit could block the airway. Roll them gently onto their side into the recovery position: bend the arm nearest you at a right angle, bring the far arm across the chest and tuck the hand under the near cheek, bend the far knee and use it to roll the body toward you, then tilt the head back slightly to keep the airway open. Monitor their breathing continuously until help arrives, and be ready to start CPR if their breathing stops or becomes abnormal. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on the recovery position]
