# Snake Bite and Animal Attacks
Source: Adapted from WHO snakebite envenoming guidance and WHO rabies post-exposure guidance — authored summary, with citations per section below

## Immediate Steps After a Snake Bite
Keep the person as calm and still as possible — movement and panic speed up venom spreading through the body via the lymphatic and circulatory systems. Remove any rings, watches, or tight clothing near the bite before swelling starts. Keep the bitten limb immobilized, ideally splinted as you would for a fracture, and at or slightly below heart level. Get the person to a hospital as quickly as possible; antivenom is the only real treatment for a venomous bite, and time from bite to antivenom treatment strongly affects outcome. If possible, note the exact time of the bite to tell hospital staff. [Source: WHO guidelines for the management of snakebite]

## What Not to Do for a Snake Bite
Do not cut the wound, do not attempt to suck out venom by mouth, do not apply a tight tourniquet, do not apply ice, and do not give the person alcohol or medication without medical advice. These traditional remedies do not remove meaningful venom and can cause additional harm, including infection, tissue damage, or dangerously restricted blood flow to the limb. If possible, try to remember the snake's color, pattern, and size to describe to medical staff, but do not risk further bites trying to catch, corner, or kill it — a description or a photo taken from a safe distance is far safer and just as useful. [Source: WHO guidelines for the management of snakebite]

## Pressure Immobilization for Certain Snake Bites
For bites from snakes whose venom mainly affects the nervous system (such as some cobra and krait species found across South Asia), applying a firm — but not tourniquet-tight — pressure bandage over and beyond the bite site, then splinting the limb to prevent movement, can help slow venom spread while transport to hospital is arranged, similar to how you would immobilize a sprained joint. The bandage should be snug enough to compress but should still allow a finger to slide under it, and circulation (color, warmth, and sensation of fingers or toes beyond the bandage) must be checked regularly. This technique is less established for bites that cause severe local swelling or tissue damage (common with some viper species), where opinion is more divided, so if in doubt, prioritize immobilization and rapid transport over trying to apply pressure bandaging precisely. [Source: WHO guidelines for the management of snakebite — pressure immobilization is a recognized technique with regional and species-dependent nuances; consult local health guidance where possible]

## Signs of Serious Envenomation
Watch for swelling spreading up the limb, severe pain, difficulty breathing, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing or speaking, slurred speech, bleeding from the gums or in urine, or the person becoming drowsy or losing consciousness — these indicate the venom is affecting the body seriously and the person needs urgent hospital care with antivenom. Not all venomous bites show obvious symptoms immediately, so any bite from a snake that cannot be confidently identified as harmless should be treated as a medical emergency and taken to hospital for observation even if the person initially feels fine. [Source: WHO guidelines for the management of snakebite]

## Dog Bites and Rabies Risk
Wash any animal bite or scratch immediately and thoroughly with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes — this alone significantly reduces rabies transmission risk by physically removing virus from the wound. Apply an antiseptic such as povidone-iodine if available after washing. Do not close the wound with stitches immediately if rabies is a concern, as this can trap the virus in the tissue; let a medical professional assess whether and when to close it. Seek medical care promptly for a rabies vaccination course, especially from a bite by an unknown, stray, or wild animal, since rabies is almost always fatal once symptoms begin but is highly preventable with timely post-exposure vaccination — do not wait to see if the animal or the wound "looks fine." [Source: WHO guidance on rabies post-exposure prophylaxis]

## Insect Stings and Allergic Reactions
For a bee or wasp sting, remove the stinger if visible by scraping it out sideways with a fingernail or card rather than squeezing it with fingers or tweezers, which can inject more venom from the attached venom sac. Apply a cold compress to reduce swelling and pain. Watch closely for signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis): difficulty breathing, swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat, widespread hives, dizziness, or fainting. Anaphylaxis is life-threatening and can progress within minutes, needing emergency care immediately; if the person has a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector, help them use it without delay and still get them to a hospital afterward, since symptoms can return. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on insect stings and anaphylaxis]

## Monkey and Wild Animal Encounters
Do not run from or make direct eye contact with an aggressive monkey or wild animal, as this can provoke a chase or attack response. Back away slowly, avoid sudden movements, and do not carry or display visible food, which is a common trigger for monkey aggression in areas where monkeys are used to being fed by people. If bitten or scratched, treat it the same as a dog bite: wash thoroughly for a full 15 minutes and seek medical care for possible rabies exposure, since monkeys, like other mammals, can carry and transmit rabies. [Source: WHO guidance on rabies post-exposure prophylaxis, general wildlife encounter safety guidance]

## Scorpion Stings
Scorpion stings cause immediate, often severe local pain, redness, and swelling, and in some species can cause more serious symptoms including sweating, restlessness, vomiting, muscle twitching, or effects on heart rate and breathing, particularly in young children and the elderly. Clean the sting site, apply a cold compress to ease pain, and keep the person calm and the affected limb still. Seek medical care, especially for a child, an elderly person, or anyone developing symptoms beyond local pain, since some regions have scorpion species medically significant enough to require antivenom or hospital monitoring. [Source: WHO general first aid guidance on venomous stings]
