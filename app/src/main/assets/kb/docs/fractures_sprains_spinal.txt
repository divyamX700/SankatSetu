# Fractures, Sprains, and Spinal Injury
Source: Adapted from ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines and WHO basic first aid guidance — authored summary, with citations per section below

## Recognizing a Possible Fracture
Suspect a broken bone if there is severe pain, swelling, visible deformity, inability to move or bear weight on the limb, bruising, or a grating sensation or sound when the limb moves. Do not try to straighten a deformed limb or force it back into position, since this can cause further damage to nerves, blood vessels, and surrounding tissue. Do not let the person eat or drink in case surgery is needed later, since anesthesia is safer on an empty stomach. Compare the injured limb with the uninjured side if possible, as an obvious size or shape difference is a useful sign even without an X-ray. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on recognizing fractures]

## Immobilizing a Fracture
Keep the injured limb still and support it in the position it was found, using padding and a splint (a straight, rigid object such as a stick, rolled newspaper, folded cardboard, or piece of wood) bound with cloth strips above and below the injury site, not directly over it. Pad the splint well, especially at bony points like the wrist or ankle, to prevent pressure injury. For an arm injury, a sling made from a folded cloth, shirt, or dupatta tied around the neck can support the weight of the arm and reduce movement. For a suspected leg fracture, you can also bind the injured leg gently to the uninjured leg for support using several ties along its length, using the good leg as an improvised splint if no rigid material is available. Check circulation regularly after splinting: bindings should not be so tight that they cut off circulation — fingers or toes beyond the splint should stay warm, keep their normal color, and regain color quickly after being pressed and released. If a limb below a splint becomes cold, pale, blue, numb, or increasingly painful, loosen the bindings immediately. Move the person to medical care as gently and quickly as possible, keeping the splinted limb elevated where practical to reduce swelling. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on splinting and fracture immobilization]

## Suspected Spinal Injury
Suspect a spinal injury after a fall from height, a road accident, a diving accident, a heavy object falling on someone, or any severe trauma to the head, neck, or back — especially if the person reports numbness, tingling, weakness, loss of bladder or bowel control, or cannot move their limbs. Do not move the person unless there is immediate danger such as fire, floodwater, or structural collapse. If you must move them in a true emergency, keep the head, neck, and spine aligned as a single unit with several people helping (a coordinated "log roll" onto a rigid surface such as a door or board), rather than bending, twisting, or lifting under the arms alone. Keep the person still, calm, and warm while waiting for trained emergency responders, who carry cervical collars and rigid backboards designed to move a spinal injury safely; if the person is wearing a helmet, do not remove it unless it is blocking their airway or you are trained to do so, since removal itself can move the neck dangerously. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on suspected spinal injury]

## Sprains and Strains
For a sprained ankle, wrist, or joint without signs of a full fracture, follow the R.I.C.E. approach: Rest the joint and avoid putting weight on it; Ice the area with a cloth-wrapped cold pack for 15-20 minutes at a time, repeated every few hours in the first day, to reduce swelling; Compress gently with an elastic bandage wrapped snugly but not so tight it restricts blood flow, numbness, or color; Elevate the limb above heart level when possible, especially in the first 24 to 48 hours. If pain is severe, the joint looks visibly deformed, there is a pop felt at the time of injury, or the person cannot bear any weight at all, treat it as a possible fracture instead and seek medical care rather than assuming it is only a sprain. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on sprains and soft tissue injury]

## Head Injury Warning Signs
After any head injury, watch for worsening headache, repeated vomiting, confusion or unusual drowsiness, one pupil larger than the other, seizures, clear or blood-tinged fluid coming from the nose or ears, weakness on one side of the body, slurred speech, or loss of consciousness even briefly — any of these needs urgent medical evaluation. Keep the person still, monitor their breathing and responsiveness at regular intervals, and do not give them food, drink, or pain medication until they have been checked by a medical professional, since some pain medications can increase bleeding risk and mask worsening symptoms. Even a head injury that initially seems mild deserves close observation for the next 24 hours, since symptoms of a serious brain injury can develop gradually. [Source: WHO/ICRC first aid guidance on head injury warning signs]

## Fractures and Injuries in Children and Elderly Persons
Children's bones are more flexible than adult bones and can suffer a partial "greenstick" fracture that bends without fully breaking, which may show only mild swelling and reluctance to use the limb rather than obvious deformity — treat any limb a young child refuses to use normally after an injury as a possible fracture and seek assessment. Elderly people have more brittle bones and a higher risk of fracture from even a minor fall, particularly of the hip and wrist, and are also more likely to be on medications that increase bleeding or affect balance; support any suspected fracture in an elderly person carefully and arrange transport rather than encouraging them to try to walk it off. [Source: general pediatric and geriatric first aid considerations, ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines]

## Dislocations
A dislocated joint (commonly the shoulder, finger, or kneecap) looks visibly out of place and is extremely painful, often with the person holding the limb rigidly in an abnormal position. Do not attempt to push, pull, or "pop" a dislocated joint back into place yourself, since this can damage surrounding nerves and blood vessels if done incorrectly. Support the joint in the position it is most comfortable for the person using padding or a sling, apply a cold compress if available to limit swelling, and get the person to medical care for proper reduction and assessment. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on dislocations]
