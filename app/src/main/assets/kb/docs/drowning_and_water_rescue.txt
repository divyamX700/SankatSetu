# Drowning and Water Rescue
Source: Adapted from ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines, WHO drowning prevention guidance, and general water rescue safety principles — authored summary, with citations per section below

## Reach, Throw, Don't Go
If someone is struggling in water, the safest rescue for an untrained person is to avoid entering the water yourself. First try to reach them with a stick, branch, rope, or piece of clothing from the shore, a boat, or a stable structure. If that is not possible, throw them something that floats — a rope, empty sealed container, ball, plastic jerry can, or life jacket — so they have something to hold onto while help arrives. Only enter the water yourself as an absolute last resort and only if you are a strong, trained swimmer familiar with the water conditions, since many drowning deaths happen when a would-be rescuer also gets into trouble and both people need saving. If a boat is available and you are trained to use it, approach from a position that lets the person hold the side rather than pulling them over the bow, which can capsize a small boat. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines and general water rescue safety principles ("reach, throw, don't go")]

## After Pulling Someone From Water
Once the person is out of the water, check if they are breathing and responsive. If they are not breathing normally, begin CPR immediately — for a drowning victim, if you are trained, start with a few rescue breaths before chest compressions, since lack of oxygen is usually the primary problem rather than a heart rhythm issue. Continue CPR until they start breathing on their own, emergency help arrives and takes over, or you cannot physically continue. Do not waste time trying to drain water from the lungs by turning the person upside down or pressing on the abdomen — this is not effective and delays the CPR that is actually needed, and can cause vomiting and choking. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on post-rescue drowning care]

## Secondary Drowning Awareness
Even a person who coughs up water and seems fine after a near-drowning incident should be monitored closely for several hours and seen by a doctor if possible. Water inhaled into the lungs can irritate lung tissue and cause breathing difficulty or complications hours later, sometimes described as delayed pulmonary symptoms after near-drowning — watch for coughing, difficulty breathing, extreme tiredness, chest pain, or behavior changes and seek medical care promptly if these appear, even if the person initially walked away seemingly unaffected. [Source: general medical guidance on near-drowning follow-up care]

## Preventing Drowning
Never leave young children unsupervised near any water, including buckets, bathtubs, ponds, wells, and pools — drowning can happen silently in seconds with very little water and without the splashing or shouting people often expect. In flood or open water situations, wear a life jacket if one is available, including a improvised flotation aid if a proper life jacket is not available. Avoid swimming alone, in unfamiliar water, at night, or after consuming alcohol, all of which are consistently linked with higher drowning risk. Be aware of strong currents near riverbanks, canals, and coastlines, especially during and after heavy rain when water levels and speed can change quickly. [Source: WHO drowning prevention guidance]

## Ice and Cold Water Immersion
Cold water immersion can cause a sudden gasping reflex and rapid loss of muscle control within minutes, even for strong swimmers, making cold water rescues especially dangerous for untrained rescuers. If someone falls through ice, do not walk out onto the ice yourself; use the reach-throw-don't-go approach with a rope, branch, or long pole from solid ground, and get emergency help immediately, since hypothermia sets in quickly and can impair the person's ability to hold on or help themselves. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines, general cold water safety principles]

## River, Flood, and Monsoon-Season Water Rescue Considerations
In flowing river or floodwater, currents are often far stronger than they appear from the bank, and depth and the ground underneath can change suddenly, including hidden drops, submerged debris, or washed-out road surfaces. Never attempt to wade into fast-moving water to reach someone, even in water that looks only knee-deep, since moving water this shallow can still sweep an adult off their feet. During monsoon season, be especially cautious around swollen rivers, canals, and low-lying areas where a normally safe crossing point can become dangerous within hours after upstream rainfall, even if it is not raining locally at that moment. [Source: NDMA India flood safety guidance, general water rescue safety principles]

## Boating and Watercraft Safety
When traveling by boat, especially an overloaded or informal ferry common in flood-affected or riverine areas, wear a life jacket if one is provided and encourage others to as well, distribute weight evenly, and avoid sudden movement that could destabilize a small craft. If a boat capsizes, stay with the boat if it remains afloat, since it is easier for rescuers to spot a capsized boat than a person alone in the water, and try to keep as many people together and calm as possible while awaiting rescue. [Source: general water safety and small-craft safety guidance]
