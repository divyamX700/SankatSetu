# Poisoning and Emergency Childbirth
Source: Adapted from WHO poisoning first aid guidance and WHO/ICRC basic emergency childbirth first aid principles — authored summary, with citations per section below

## Suspected Poisoning — General Steps
If you suspect someone has been poisoned (swallowed, inhaled, or absorbed through skin), first ensure your own safety before approaching, especially with gas or chemical exposure, since a rescuer overcome by the same substance cannot help anyone. Try to identify what was involved — keep the container, packaging, plant, or substance if possible to show medical staff, since this can significantly speed up correct treatment. Call emergency services or a poison control helpline immediately and follow their specific guidance, since treatment differs greatly depending on the substance involved — what helps for one poison can be harmful for another. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on poisoning]

## What Not to Do for Poisoning
Do not make the person vomit unless specifically told to by a medical professional or poison control — for some substances (like strong acids, alkalis, kerosene, or other petroleum products), vomiting can cause additional serious injury to the throat and airway on the way back up, or the fumes can be inhaled into the lungs. Do not give the person food, water, milk, or any home remedy without medical guidance, since this can also be harmful or delay proper treatment depending on the poison. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on poisoning]

## If the Person Is Unconscious from Poisoning
Check breathing and responsiveness. If they are breathing but unconscious, place them in the recovery position (on their side, head tilted back slightly, to keep the airway clear and prevent choking on vomit) and monitor closely until help arrives. If they are not breathing normally, begin CPR. Get emergency help immediately in either case, and tell responders what substance you believe was involved if you know. [Source: WHO/ICRC first aid guidance on unconscious poisoning cases]

## Common Poisoning Situations in Rural and Household Settings
Common sources of poisoning include agricultural pesticides and fertilizers, kerosene and other fuels stored in unlabeled containers, household cleaning chemicals, spoiled or contaminated food, and certain plants or mushrooms. Store all chemicals and pesticides in their original, clearly labeled containers, out of reach of children, and never in a container normally used for food or drink, since this is a leading cause of accidental poisoning, particularly in children who cannot tell the difference by looking. If pesticide poisoning is suspected, avoid direct skin contact with the person's contaminated clothing when helping them, and remove contaminated clothing while protecting your own hands. [Source: WHO first aid guidance on poisoning, general pesticide safety guidance]

## Emergency Childbirth — When Birth Cannot Wait
If a birth is happening and there is no time to reach a hospital, stay calm and call for medical help or guidance by phone if any connection is available, even if you cannot travel to a facility. Have the mother lie down in a comfortable position, ideally on a clean surface with clean cloths nearby, and wash your hands with soap and water if at all possible before assisting. Let the birth happen naturally — do not pull on the baby. Support the baby's head and body gently as it emerges; babies are slippery, so hold securely with both hands, supporting the head and neck first. [Source: WHO basic emergency childbirth first aid principles]

## Immediately After the Baby is Born
Dry the baby gently and thoroughly with a clean cloth, which also helps stimulate breathing, and keep them warm, skin-to-skin with the mother if possible, covered together with a blanket including the baby's head. Most babies cry and start breathing within moments; if a baby does not seem to be breathing after drying and stimulation, gently rub their back and the soles of their feet to stimulate them further, and seek urgent medical help if they still do not breathe. Do not cut the umbilical cord unless you have clean, sterile equipment and know how to do so safely — it is generally safe to leave it attached and wait for medical help, keeping the placenta at the same level as the baby if it has not yet delivered, rather than pulling on the cord. [Source: WHO basic emergency childbirth first aid principles]

## Caring for Mother After Birth
The placenta will usually deliver within 30 minutes after the baby; do not pull on the umbilical cord to hurry this along, as it can cause serious bleeding or leave placental tissue behind. Watch for heavy bleeding after birth (soaking through cloth rapidly, more than one pad or cloth every 15 minutes) — this needs urgent medical attention, as postpartum hemorrhage is a leading cause of maternal death and can happen even after an otherwise straightforward birth. Keep the mother warm, encourage her to rest, offer fluids if she is alert and able to swallow, and get both mother and baby to a medical facility for proper checkup as soon as possible, even if the birth seemed to go smoothly, since both need postnatal assessment. [Source: WHO basic emergency childbirth first aid principles]

## If the Baby Arrives Before Help and Complications Are Suspected
If the baby appears very small, is born noticeably early, is not crying or moving after stimulation, or the umbilical cord is wrapped around the baby's neck, stay calm: gently check whether the cord can be slipped over the baby's head or shoulder without force, keep the baby warm and dry, and continue stimulation while getting emergency help as fast as possible — do not attempt to cut a tightly wrapped cord yourself without sterile equipment and training. If the mother experiences seizures, severe headache with vision changes, or loses consciousness before or after birth, treat this as a medical emergency requiring the fastest possible transport to a hospital, since these can indicate a serious pregnancy-related condition. [Source: WHO basic emergency childbirth first aid principles, general obstetric emergency awareness]
