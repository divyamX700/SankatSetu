# Hypothermia and Cold Exposure
Source: Adapted from WHO cold-health guidance and ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines — authored summary, with citations per section below

## Recognizing Hypothermia
Hypothermia is when body temperature drops dangerously low, most often from cold weather, wet clothing, wind exposure, or cold water immersion. Early signs include shivering, tiredness, confusion, and clumsiness. As it worsens, shivering may actually stop (a dangerous sign, not a good one, since the body has exhausted its ability to generate heat this way), and the person may become very confused, slur speech, move slowly and stumble, or lose consciousness. Severe hypothermia is life-threatening and needs emergency care as fast as possible. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on hypothermia recognition]

## Treating Hypothermia
Move the person to a warm, dry place and out of the wind as soon as possible. Remove any wet clothing and replace with dry, warm layers or blankets, covering the head as well since significant heat is lost from an uncovered head and neck. Warm the person gradually with body heat (lying close together under blankets), warm — not hot — dry compresses to the neck, chest, and groin where blood vessels run close to the skin, and warm sweet drinks if they are fully alert and able to swallow safely — never give alcohol, which increases heat loss from the body despite feeling warming. Handle a severely hypothermic person very gently, avoiding rough movement or vigorous rubbing of limbs, since this can trigger a dangerous heart rhythm in a cold heart; get them to emergency medical care as soon as possible. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on hypothermia treatment]

## Frostbite
Frostbite is localized freezing of skin and underlying tissue, most common on fingers, toes, ears, and nose. Affected skin may look white, greyish, or waxy and feel numb or hard to the touch. Do not rub or massage frostbitten skin, since ice crystals in the tissue can cause further damage, and do not use direct high heat like an open fire or heater, as numb tissue can burn without the person feeling it happening. Instead, warm the area gradually in warm — not hot — water around normal body temperature, and seek medical care, especially if numbness persists after rewarming or blisters form, since deep frostbite can require specialist treatment. Avoid refreezing a thawed area, as repeated freeze-thaw cycles cause worse tissue damage than a single freeze. [Source: ICRC/IFRC first aid guidelines on frostbite]

## Preventing Cold Exposure Emergencies
Dress in layers, keeping an outer layer that blocks wind and moisture, since wind chill and wet clothing both accelerate heat loss far more than still, dry cold air. Keep hands, feet, ears, and head well covered, since these extremities and the head lose heat fastest. Stay dry — wet clothing conducts heat away from the body far faster than dry clothing, so change out of wet clothes as soon as practical. If travelling in cold conditions, especially in hills or at night, tell someone your route and expected return time, and carry emergency supplies including extra dry clothing, food, and a way to signal for help. [Source: WHO cold-health guidance / ICRC first aid guidelines]

## Cold Exposure in Infants, Elderly, and People Outdoors Overnight
Infants and elderly people lose body heat faster and generate less heat through shivering than healthy adults, making them more vulnerable to hypothermia even in conditions that feel only mildly cold to others; watch an infant for cold, mottled skin, unusual quietness, or reluctance to feed, and check on elderly relatives and neighbours during cold spells, especially those living alone with limited heating. People sleeping outdoors or in unheated shelters during winter, including those displaced after a disaster, are at particular risk overnight when temperatures drop most — prioritize warm, dry bedding, blocking drafts, and grouping people together for shared body heat where shelter capacity allows. [Source: WHO cold-health guidance, general vulnerable-population cold safety considerations]

## Hypothermia from Combined Cold and Wet Conditions (Monsoon and Flood Settings)
Prolonged exposure to cold rain or standing in floodwater can cause hypothermia even in a climate that is not generally considered cold, because wet skin and clothing lose heat much faster than dry conditions at the same air temperature. People stranded in floodwater, especially for extended periods, overnight, or during cyclone-related rain, should get out of wet clothing and into dry, warm coverings as soon as they reach safety, even if the ambient temperature does not feel extreme. [Source: general first aid guidance on cold-wet exposure, NDMA India flood safety guidance]
